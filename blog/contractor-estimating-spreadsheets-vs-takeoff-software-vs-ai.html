Contractor estimating: spreadsheets vs. takeoff software vs. AI estimators

Jacob Fischer · Remodeling contractor, Revisions Unlimited LLC · August 27, 2026

There are three real ways contractors build estimates today. Spreadsheets are nearly free and infinitely flexible, but slow, manual, and easy to break. Takeoff software measures accurately from drawings, but carries a real learning curve and assumes drawings exist — which most renovations don't have. AI estimators are the fastest for as-built remodel work because they measure from photos, but their drafts need your judgment before they go to a client. The right choice depends on your job mix, your volume, and how much your evenings are worth. Here's the honest breakdown of all three — including where each one falls down.

Option 1: Spreadsheets — cheap, flexible, slow

Every estimator starts here, and plenty of good ones never leave. A well-built spreadsheet with standardized line items and a maintained price book is a legitimate estimating system, and building one teaches you more about your costs than any software will.

What it costs: essentially nothing beyond the office suite you already pay for.

Where it wins: total control over structure and math; no learning curve if you already live in Excel or Google Sheets; your price book is exactly your numbers; works offline in a truck.

Where it hurts: every measurement is manual — the spreadsheet prices the takeoff, it doesn't do the takeoff. Formulas rot quietly as the file gets copied job to job, and a broken cell reference can misprice a bid without any visible error. It's single-user by nature, version control is "Final_v3_REAL.xlsx", and the client-facing output is a spreadsheet export, not a proposal. Most importantly, it does nothing about the evening-estimate burden — the three hours per bid stay three hours.

Option 2: Takeoff software — accurate, plan-based, steep

Dedicated takeoff and estimating platforms let you measure digitally off blueprints and PDF plan sets — click the corners, get the quantities, feed them into cost databases. For plan-based work, this category is genuinely excellent.

What it costs: typically a per-seat monthly or annual subscription, commonly running from tens to hundreds of dollars per month depending on the platform and tier — plus the real cost, which is training time.

Where it wins: precise quantities from drawings; strong for new construction, additions with architect plans, and commercial bid sets; integrates with cost databases and bid workflows; scales to dedicated estimating departments.

Where it hurts: the learning curve is measured in weeks, not minutes — these are professional tools that assume estimating is a big part of your job. And the deeper problem for remodelers: takeoff software measures drawings, and a 1960s bathroom gut has no drawings. The existing house is the plan. For as-built renovation work you end up measuring on site by hand anyway, which quietly deletes the tool's core advantage.

Option 3: AI estimators — fast, as-built, needs review

The newest category works from reality instead of drawings: photos and a short walkthrough video of the actual space go in, and a drafted estimate comes out. RenoBeam is built this way — it measures the space from your capture, itemizes labor and materials with quantities, applies regional pricing, and produces a branded, client-ready PDF proposal in minutes.

What it will cost: RenoBeam plans to launch at $79/month (Solo), $199/month (Crew, with 5 seats and branded proposals), or $499/month (Firm, unlimited with API access). Those are planned prices — nothing is for sale yet.

Where it wins: speed on as-built work — capture happens during the walkthrough you were doing anyway, and the takeoff, line items, and proposal formatting are done before you leave the driveway. No drawings required, no measuring hardware, no real learning curve: if you can photograph a room, you can run it.

Where it hurts — honestly: an AI can't see inside walls, so hidden conditions are still your allowance to carry. Model output must be reviewed line by line before it goes to a client; an unreviewed AI estimate is a liability, not a bid. And like any drafting tool, it's most accurate when you feed it your own numbers — which is what the price-book feature is for. Treat it as the fastest first draft you've ever had, not an oracle.

When each one fits

  • Stay on spreadsheets if you bid a handful of jobs a month, your scopes repeat, and the evening estimating time isn't costing you work yet.
  • Buy takeoff software if most of your bids come off plan sets — new builds, engineered additions, commercial work — and estimating is a core role in your company.
  • Use an AI estimator if your bread and butter is as-built renovation — kitchens, baths, basements, whole-home remodels — and slow quotes are losing you jobs (here's the math on what slow quoting costs, and the photo-estimating method the AI automates).
  • Mix them — plenty of GCs run takeoff software for ground-up work and an AI estimator for renovation scopes. The tools aren't rivals so much as tools for different jobs.

One factor is constant across all three: pricing is local. A price book tuned for California or New York City will overprice work in Nashville, and a Seattle labor rate makes no sense in rural markets. Spreadsheets make you maintain that yourself; RenoBeam's regional adjustment handles the baseline and lets your price book override it.

FAQ: choosing an estimating approach

Can I keep my spreadsheet price book if I switch to an AI estimator?

Yes — and you should. Your price book is years of real job data. RenoBeam's price-book customization (on Crew and Firm plans) lets you load your own labor rates and material costs, so the AI drafts with your numbers instead of generic baselines.

Is takeoff software overkill for remodeling work?

Often, yes. Takeoff software is built to measure drawings, and most remodels don't have drawings — the existing house is the plan. If your work is mostly plan-based, it earns its cost. For as-built renovation work, you end up doing the measuring yourself anyway.

Are AI estimates accurate enough to send to clients?

As a reviewed draft, yes; unreviewed, no. The AI does the measuring and line-item drafting; you apply judgment on hidden conditions, access, and code before sending. Every line in RenoBeam is editable, and the review typically takes minutes rather than hours.

What's the cheapest way to start estimating properly?

A spreadsheet with standardized line items and a real price book — it costs nearly nothing and teaches you how estimates work.
